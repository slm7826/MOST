\section{Basic equations of motion}

Following \cite{Monin1971}, start with the basic equations of motion in Boussinesq approximation:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align} \label{eq:boussinesq}
   \pderiv{\bm{u}}{t} + (\bm{u} \cdot \nabla) \bm{u} & = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\nabla{p'} + \nu \nabla^2 \bm{u} - \frac{\bm{g}}{p_0}\pt' \\
   \pderiv{\pt}{t} + (\bm{u} \cdot \nabla) \bm{\pt} & =  \chi\nabla^2 \pt
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and continuity equation in approximation of incompressible liquid
\begin{equation}
   \nabla \cdot \bm{u} = 0
\end{equation}
where $\pt$ is potential temperature, $\pt'$ is the departure from mean, and $p'$ is the departure from hydrostatics.
Viscosity terms in the right-hand side are typically neglected.

Fluxes of momentum and heat within constant flux layer
\begin{gather}
   \tau = \rho_0 (\overline{u'w'}) = \text{const}_1 \\
   H = \rho_0 c_p (\overline{\pt'w'}) = \text{const}_2
\end{gather}

Friction velocity scale $\ustar$ is defined by the momentum sink at the surface,
\begin{equation}
   \tau = \rho_0 (\overline{u'w'}) = \rho_0 \ustar^2
\end{equation}

Similarly, temperature scale $\tstar$ is defined by the relationship
\begin{equation}
   H = \rho_0 c_p (\overline{\pt'w'}) = \rho_0 c_p \tstar \ustar
\end{equation}

\section{Dimensional reasoning}

\subsection{Neutral case}
In the neutral case, far from the wall (where the viscosity effects can be important) the vertical gradient of the mean wind can only depend on stress $\tau$, air density $\rho$, and vertical coordinate $z$ \cite[see][section 5.3]{Monin1971}, therefore the equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
   \pderiv{u}{z} = \frac{\ustar}{\vonkarman z}
\end{equation}

Per \cite{Monin1971}, the dimensionless von Karman constant $\vonkarman$ was first introduced into the equation for $L$ by Obukhov in 1946 and has usually been preserved in all subsequent works by tradition. $L>0$ for stable situations.

\subsection{Stratified flow}
The statistics of the flow in the near-surface layer can only depend on small number of parameters that appear in the above section, namely on temperature-to-buoyancy scale $g/\pt_0$, density $\rho_0$, fluxes of momentum $\tau$ (or, equivalently, velocity scale $\ustar$) and temperature $H/(\rho_0 c_p)=\overline{\pt'w'}$, and $z$. Units of these quantities are
\begin{equation}
    \left[\frac{g}{\pt_0}\right] = \si{\per\K\m\per\s\squared}; \quad
    \left[\rho_0\right] = \si{\kg\per\m\cubed}; \quad
    \left[\ustar\right] = \si{\m\per\s}; \quad
    \left[\overline{\pt'w'}\right] = \si{\K\m\per\s}
\end{equation}

There is only one way (to the arbitrary power) to combine the above parameters to construct the values of length dimension:
\begin{equation}
    \left[\frac{g}{\pt_0}\right]^{n_1}
    \left[\rho_0\right]^{n_2}
    \left[\ustar\right]^{n_3}
    \left[\overline{\pt'w'}\right]^{n_4} = [z]
\end{equation}
and that is
\begin{equation}
   L = - \frac{\ustar^3}{\vonkarman\frac{g}{\pt_0}(\overline{\pt'w'})}
     = \frac{\ustar^2}{\vonkarman\frac{g}{\pt_0}\tstar}
\end{equation}
%
$L$ is negative for unstable cases, positive in stable conditions, infinite in neutral.

In non-neutral case,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:u-profile}
   \frac{\vonkarman z}{\ustar}\pderiv{u}{z} = \fstab_m(\zeta)
\end{equation}
where
$\zeta=z/L$,
$\fstab_m$ is stability correction function,
$\fstab_m \to 1$ as $\zeta \to 0$ ro retrieve neutral case.

\cite{Held2004} then defines
\label{eq:Held-integral}
\begin{equation}
   F_i(\zeta) = \int\frac{\fstab_i}{\zeta}\,d \zeta \\
\end{equation}
with $i=m$ for momentum and $h$ for heat, to solve \eqref{eq:u-profile} as
\begin{equation}
   u(z) = \frac{\ustar}{\vonkarman} \left[
          F_m\left(\frac{z}{L}\right)-F_m\left(\frac{\zm}{L}\right)
      \right]
\end{equation}

Other literature, including \cite{Jimenez2012} use equivalent approach to define
\begin{equation}
   \psi_i(\zeta) = \int\frac{1-\fstab_i(\zeta)}{\zeta}\, d \zeta
               = \ln(\zeta) - F_i(\zeta)
\end{equation}
to get
\begin{equation}
   u(z) = \frac{\ustar}{\vonkarman}\left[
          \ln\left(\frac{z}{\zm}\right) - \psi_m\left(\frac{z}{L}\right) + \psi_m\left(\frac{\zm}{L}\right)
      \right]
\end{equation}

Similarly, for temperature
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b-profile}
   \frac{\vonkarman z}{\tstar}\pderiv{\pt}{z} = \fstab_h(\zeta)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
   \pt(z) - \pt(0) = \frac{\tstar}{\vonkarman} \left[
          F_h\left(\frac{z}{L}\right)-F_h\left(\frac{\zh}{L}\right)
      \right]
\end{equation}
or
\begin{equation}
   \pt(z) - \pt(0) = \frac{\tstar}{\vonkarman}\left[
          \ln\left(\frac{z}{\zh}\right) - \psi_h\left(\frac{z}{L}\right) + \psi_h\left(\frac{\zh}{L}\right)
      \right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Turbulent diffusion coefficients}
By definition of turbulent diffusion coefficient
\begin{equation}
   (\overline{u'w'}) = K_m \pderiv{u}{z} = \ustar^2
\end{equation}
and substituting ${\partial u/\partial z}$ from~\eqref{eq:u-profile}
\begin{equation}
   K_m = \frac{\ustar^2}{\partial u/\partial z} = \frac{\ustar \vonkarman z}{\fstab_m(\zeta)}
\end{equation}

\section{Stability functions}
Current FMS code (2020.04) can use a number of stability correction functions, controlled by options specified in \verb|monin_obukhov_nml|.

\subsection{Stable option 1}
In unstable regime ($\zeta < 0$), the stability functions are defined as
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:fms-unstable}
\begin{align}
   \fstab_m(\zeta) & = (1-16 \zeta)^{-1/4} \\
   \fstab_h(\zeta) & = \begin{cases}
      (1-16\zeta)^{-1/3}, & \text{for new option (Ming Zhao)} \\
      (1-16\zeta)^{-1/2}, & \text{for old option}
   \end{cases}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Note that the momentum stability function is the same as in \cite{Held2004}

In stable case (for $\zeta>0$)
\begin{equation}
   \fstab_m(\zeta) = \fstab_h(\zeta) = 1 + \left(5 + \frac{\zeta}{\rcrit}\right)\frac{\zeta}{1+\zeta} \\
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stable option 2}
In unstable regime, stability corrections are the same as in stable option 1, equations~\eqref{eq:fms-unstable}. In stable regime
\begin{equation}
   \fstab_m(\zeta) = \fstab_h(\zeta) = \begin{cases}
      1+5\zeta, & \zeta < \zeta_t \\
      1+5 \zeta_t + \frac{\zeta-\zeta_t}{\rcrit}, & \zeta \ge \zeta_t
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note that in stable case, $\fstab_m(\zeta) = \fstab_h(\zeta)$, like they were in \cite{Held2004}.

\subsection{Brutsaert}
Stability function proposed in \cite{Brutsaert2005} was introduced in the FMS code on branch \verb|usr/slm/sfcx|, together with refactoring of the Monin-Obukhov code to make it more easily expandable and manageable.

For unstable case
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \fstab_m(\zeta) & = \begin{cases}
        (a_u+b_u y^{4/3})/(a_u + y), & y \le b_u^{-3} \\
        1,                            & y > b_u^{-3}
    \end{cases}
    \\
    \fstab_h(\zeta) & = (c_u + d_u y^{n_u})/(c_u+y^{n_u})
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $y=-\zeta$, $a_u = 0.33$, $b_u = 0.41$, $c_u = 0.33$, $d_u = 0.057$, and $n_u = 0.78$.

In stable case
\begin{equation}
    \fstab_m(\zeta) = \fstab_h(\zeta)
        = 1 + a_s \frac{\zeta + \zeta^{b_s}(1+\zeta^{b_s})^{-1+1/b_s}}
                       {\zeta + (1+\zeta^{b_s})^{1/b_s}}
\end{equation}
with $a_s = 6.1$ and  $b_s = 2.5$.

The complicated form of stable case correction $\fstab_x$ is because a relatively simple integral stability function ws postulated first
\begin{equation}
\Psi_x = -a_s \ln\big[\zeta + (1+\zeta^{b_s})^{1/b_s}\big]
\end{equation}
and then then stability corrections $\fstab_x$ were obtained by differentiation.

\section{Solving for surface fluxes}
Following \cite{Held2004} note, bulk Richardson number can be calculated from known winds and virtual potential temperatures at the top of the surface boundary layer $z_a$ and the surface as
\begin{equation}
    \rbulk = z_a\frac{b(z_a)-b(0)}{u(z_a)^2} = g z_a \frac{\theta_v(z_a)-\theta_v(0)}{\theta_v(0)u(z_a)^2}
\end{equation}
where
$z_a$ is the height of the height of the lowest atmospheric layer, and  $\zeta_a = z_a/L$.

On the other hand, the same bulk Richardson function can be expressed in terms of integral stability functions~\eqref{eq:Held-integral}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ribulk}
    \rbulk = \zeta \frac{F_h(\zeta_a) - F_h(\zeta_a \zh/z_a)}
        {(F_m(\zeta_a) - F_m(\zeta_a \zm/z_a))^2}
\end{equation}
where
$\zm$ is the roughness length of momentum,
$\zh$ -- roughness length for buoyancy,
and
$\zeta_a = z_a/L$.

Defining, for convenience, function
\begin{equation}
    F_i(\zeta_1, \zeta_2) = F_i(\zeta_2) - F_i(\zeta_1)
                          = \int_{\zeta_1}^{\zeta_2} \frac{\fstab_i(\zeta)}{\zeta} d \zeta
\end{equation}
we can rewrite~\eqref{eq:ribulk} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta}
    f(\zeta_a) = \zeta_a \frac{F_h(\zeta_a \zh/z_a, \zeta_a)}{F_m(\zeta_a \zm/z_a,\zeta_a)^2} - \rbulk = 0
\end{equation}

FMS code solves nonlinear equation~\eqref{eq:zeta} iteratively, to find the value of $\zeta_a = z_a/L$ that match boundary conditions. The solution procedure follows the Newton method, so on each iteration step, given a guess of $\zeta_0$, the step $\Delta \zeta$ is calculated as
\begin{equation}
   f(\zeta_0) + \fderiv{f}{\zeta} \Delta \zeta = 0
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
   \Delta \zeta = - f(\zeta_0)\Big[\fderiv{f}{\zeta}\Big]^{-1}
\end{equation}
until its absolute value falls below predefined tolerance.

Noting that
\begin{equation}
    \pderiv{F_i(\zeta_1,\zeta_2)}{\zeta_1} = - \frac{\fstab_i(\zeta_1)}{\zeta_1}, \qquad
    \pderiv{F_i(\zeta_1,\zeta_2)}{\zeta_2} = \frac{\fstab_i(\zeta_2)}{\zeta_2}
\end{equation}
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\fderiv{f}{\zeta}
    & =  \frac{F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a, \zeta)}{F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)^2}
    + \frac{\fstab_h(\zeta)-\fstab_h(\zeta \zh/z_a)}{F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)^2}
    - 2 \frac{F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a, \zeta)}{F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)^3}(\fstab_m(\zeta)-\fstab_m(\zeta \zm/z_a))
    \label{eq:Fderiv1}
    \\
    {} &=  \zeta\frac{F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a, \zeta)}{F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)^2}
        \Bigg[
            \frac{1}{\zeta}
            + \frac{\fstab_h(\zeta)-\fstab_h(\zeta \zh/z_a)}{\zeta F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a,\zeta)}
            - 2 \frac{\fstab_m(\zeta)-\fstab_m(\zeta \zm/z_a)}{\zeta F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)}
        \Bigg]
    \label{eq:Fderiv2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\begin{mdframed}
An alternative mathematically equivalent form of equation~\eqref{eq:zeta} would be
\begin{equation}
    f_1(\zeta) = \zeta F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a, \zeta) - \rbulk F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta)^2 = 0
\end{equation}
so that the derivative of the function would be simpler:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    \fderiv{f_1}{\zeta} & = F_h(\zeta \zh/z_a, \zeta) + \fstab_h(\zeta)-\fstab_h(\zeta \zh/z_a) \\
    & \qquad \qquad - 2 \rbulk F_m(\zeta \zm/z_a,\zeta) \frac{1}{\zeta} [\fstab_m(\zeta)-\fstab_m(\zeta \zm/z_a)]
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
This is not the expression that the code is using, though. Not sure why, it looks simpler.
The only disadvantage that I can see is that it includes $1/\zeta$ term that may cause problems at $\zeta=0$; the equation~\eqref{eq:Fderiv1} does not, although the code uses the form~\eqref{eq:Fderiv2}.
\end{mdframed}

\section{Free convection limit}

As $u \to 0$, $\ustar \to 0$, and $\zeta \to -\infty$. In this case, vertical profile of temperature may become ill defined, depending on the asymptotic properties of the stability functions. Consequently, vertical flux of buoyancy $B$ becomes ill defined.

To see what is going on, start from equation for vertical profile of buoyancy \eqref{eq:b-profile}:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\vonkarman z}{\bstar}\pderiv{b}{z} = \fstab_h(\zeta)
\end{equation*}
Also, from definition of \bstar~\eqref{eq:bstar}
\begin{equation*}
    \bstar = \frac{B}{\rho \ustar}
\end{equation*}
and definition of $\zeta$
\begin{equation*}
    \zeta = \frac{z}{L} = -\frac{z \vonkarman \bstar}{\ustar^2}
\end{equation*}
we get
\begin{equation}
    \pderiv{b}{z} = \frac{\bstar}{\vonkarman z}\fstab_h(\zeta)
    = \frac{\bstar}{\vonkarman z}\fstab_h\left(-\frac{z \vonkarman \bstar}{\ustar^2}\right)
    = \frac{B}{\vonkarman z \rho \ustar }\fstab_h
      \left(-\frac{z \vonkarman B}{\rho \ustar^3}\right)
\end{equation}

When the wind is close to zero in non-neutral situation, it is reasonable to assume that the heat flux should not depend on $\ustar$. Therefore, in equation~\eqref{eq:b-profile}, the dependence on $\ustar$ should disappear when wind goes to zero (and, therefore, $\ustar \to 0$ and $\zeta \to \infty$). Suppose $\fstab(\zeta) \propto \zeta^n$ for $\zeta \to -\infty$, with some power $n$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{B}{\vonkarman z \rho \ustar }\fstab_h \left(-\frac{z \vonkarman B}{\rho \ustar^3}\right)
  = \frac{B}{\vonkarman z \rho \ustar } C \left(-\frac{z \vonkarman B}{\rho \ustar^3}\right)^n
  = - C \frac{B^{n+1} z^{n-1} \vonkarman^{n-1}}{\rho^{n+1}} \frac{1}{\ustar^{3n+1}}
\end{equation}
For this expression to be finite and non-zero when $\ustar \to 0$, we should have $n=-1/3$.

For $n < -1/3$, the vertical gradient of buoyancy would tend to zero for finite heat flux $B$, which implies that the heat flux would tend to infinity for finite values of vertical gradient. On the other hand, if $n > -1/3$, the vertical gradient would tend to infinity for finite $B$.

\cite{Held2004} and \cite{Beljaars1994} propose to use ``gustiness'' to solve this problem: adding an ortogonal component to the atmospheric wind speed, determined by the $\bstar$, $\ustar$, and imposed parameters of the convective boundary layer:
\begin{gather}
   |U| = U^2 + V^2 + G^2 \\
   G = \beta \left(z_i \bstar \ustar \right)^{1/3}
\end{gather}
where $\beta$ is an empirical constant on the order of 1, and
$z_i$ is the characteristic height of the convective boundary layer \citep{Beljaars1994}. In GFDL models, $z_i$ is assumed to be constant \SI{1000}{m}.

This treatment should ensure the same asymptotic behavior as imposing the above constraint on $\fstab_h$ at $\ustar \to -\infty$. However, such approach can only work if calculation of gustiness is embedded within the solution of the implicit equations of heat and momentum balances in the Monin-Obukhov layer. In GFDL model that is not done: rather, gustiness is calculated in the atmosphere based on the flux and stability values from the previous time step. Therefore, I believe there is no guarantee that the surface fluxes produce reasonable solution in convective limit.

\section{Roughness sublayer corrections}

Roughness sublayer formulations need to introduce another length scale into consideration -- roughness sublayer depth $\zR$. The equation for vertical profile~\eqref{eq:u-profile} and~\eqref{eq:b-profile} then transform to
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:profile-rsl}
\begin{align}
   \frac{\vonkarman z}{\ustar}\pderiv{u}{z} & = \fstab_m(z/L)\frsl_m(z/\zR) \\
   \frac{\vonkarman z}{\tstar}\pderiv{\pt}{z} & = \fstab_h(z/L)\frsl_h(z/\zR)
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

There are multiple forms of roughness correction function suggested  in literature; for example \cite{Ridder2010} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ridder2010}
    \frsl_i(z/\zR) = 1-\exp\left(-\mu_i\frac{z}{\zR}\right)
\end{equation}
where
$i$ is either "$m$" for momentum and "$h$" for buoyancy.

Ghannam (2022) proposes somewhat different formulation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ghannam2022}
    \frsl_i(z/\zR) = 1-\mu_1 \exp\left(-\mu_i\frac{z}{\zR}\right)
\end{equation}
with $\mu_1 = 0.67$, $\mu_m = 2$, and $mu_t = 1$; he also defines $\zR$ in terms of vegetation properties.

That is, since $\frsl_i < 1$, for the same $\ustar$ and $\tstar$ roughness sublayer correction reduces the vertical gradients, meaning that it increases turbulent diffusion coefficients. The effect is obviously stronger for smaller $z$.

To obtain turbulent diffusion coefficients, one can write
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
   (\overline{u'w'}) & = K_m \pderiv{u}{z} = \ustar^2
   \\
   (\overline{\pt'w'}) & = K_h \pderiv{\pt}{z} = \ustar\tstar
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
%
and substituting ${\partial u/\partial z}$ and ${\partial \pt/\partial z}$ from~\eqref{eq:profile-rsl}
%
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
   K_m & = \frac{\ustar^2}{\partial u/\partial z} = \frac{\ustar \vonkarman z}{\fstab_m(z/L)\frsl_m(z/z_R)}
   \\
   K_h & = \frac{\ustar\tstar}{\partial \pt/\partial z} = \frac{\ustar \vonkarman z}{\fstab_h(z/L)\frsl_h(z/\zR)}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\section{Solving for surface fluxes with RSL}

Presumably the form of equations \eqref{eq:ribulk} does not change with introduction of surface roughness sublayer corrections, but the form of integral stability functions does change:
\begin{align}
  \FRSL_i(z,L,\zR) & = \int \frac{\fstab_i(z'/L)\frsl_i(z'/\zR)}{z'}  d z' \notag \\
{} & = \int \frac{\fstab_i(z'/L)}{z'}  d z'
                 - \int \frac{\fstab_i(z'/L)[1-\frsl_i(z'/\zR)]}{z'}  d z'
\end{align}

The first term of the above equation is exactly the form of integral stability function~\eqref{eq:Held-integral} in the formulation without roughness stability sublayer \citep{Held2004}.

\begin{align}
  \FRSL_i(z_1,z_2,L,\zR)
    & \bydef \FRSL_i(z_2,L,\zR) - \FRSL_i(z_1,L,\zR) \notag \\
    & = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{\fstab_i(z/L)}{z}  d z
      - \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{\fstab_i(z/L)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z}  d z \notag \\
    & = F_i(\zeta_1,\zeta_2) - \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{\fstab_i(\zeta_a z/z_a)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z}  d z
    \label{eq:FRSL}
\end{align}
where we used equivalence $\zeta_a = z_a/L$ under the integral in the last expression.
Note that for $z_1 < z_2$ the second tem of~\eqref{eq:FRSL} is positive, so that $\FRSL_i(z_1,z_2,L,\zR) < F_i(\zeta_1,\zeta_2)$.

To find $\zeta_a$ we can use the same equation for bulk Richardson number as before, except integral stability function must now include RSL correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zetaRSL}
   g(\zeta_a) =
   \zeta_a \frac{\FRSL_h(\zeta_a \zh/z_a, \zeta_a)}{\FRSL_m(\zeta_a \zm/z_a,\zeta_a)^2} - \rbulk = 0
\end{equation}

To apply the same Newton method of solution, we must estimate the derivative of the $g$ w.r.t. $\zeta_a$
\begin{align}
   \pderiv{g}{\zeta_a} & =
    \frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^2} + \zeta_a \frac{1}{\FRSL_m^2}\fderiv{\FRSL_h}{\zeta_a} - 2 \zeta_a \frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^3}\fderiv{\FRSL_m}{\zeta_a}
    \notag \\
    {} & = \frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^2} + \zeta_a \frac{1}{\FRSL_m^2}\fderiv{F_h}{\zeta_a} - 2 \zeta_a \frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^3}\fderiv{F_m}{\zeta_a}
                                  - \zeta_a \frac{1}{\FRSL_m^2}\fderiv{R_h}{\zeta_a} + 2 \zeta_a \frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^3}\fderiv{R_m}{\zeta_a}
    \notag \\
    {} & = \zeta_a\frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^2}
        \bigg[
            \frac{1}{\zeta_a}
            + \frac{\fstab_h(\zeta_a)-\fstab_h(\zeta_a \zh/z_a)}{\zeta_a \FRSL_h}
            - 2 \frac{\fstab_m(\zeta_a)-\fstab_m(\zeta_a \zm/z_a)}{\FRSL_m}
        \bigg]
    \notag \\
    {} & \qquad \qquad \qquad - \zeta_a\frac{\FRSL_h}{\FRSL_m^2}\bigg[\frac{1}{\FRSL_h}\fderiv{R_h}{\zeta_a}-2\frac{1}{\FRSL_m}\pderiv{R_m}{\zeta_a}\bigg]
    \label{eq:gderiv}
\end{align}
where we introduced notation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:integralR}
    R_i = \int_{z_{0i}}^{z_a} \frac{\fstab_i(\zeta_a z/z_a)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z}  d z
\end{equation}
for the second term in the right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:FRSL} to simplify notation. In this expression $z_{0i} = \zm$ for momentum, and $z_{0i} = \zh$ for heat.

The first part in the right-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:gderiv} has exactly the same form as in the original treatment without RSL in \eqref{eq:Fderiv2}; the derivatives of $R_m$ and $R_h$ are hard to estimate analytically, but they can be evaluated numerically.

\subsubsection{Practical estimation of integral $R_i$}
In the course of the numerical solution of equation~\eqref{eq:zetaRSL}, integrals $R_i$ \eqref{eq:integralR} must be estimated multiple times for every surface flux calculation. That makes the efficiency of this estimation an important factor. Obviously direct integration would likely to be computationally prohibitive. One could imagine tabulating the integral $R_i$ in a look-up table, but it depends on at least four parameters ($z_1$, $z_2$, $L$, and $\zR$) and a reasonably accurate look-up table would be huge.

To facilitate the lookup table approach, $R_i$ can represented as a sum of two terms:
Second term of equation~\eqref{eq:FRSL}
\begin{align}
    R_i(z_1,z_2,L,\zR)
        & = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \frac{\fstab_i(z/L)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z}  d z \notag\\
        & = \underbrace{\int_{z_1}^{\infty} \frac{\fstab_i(z/L)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z} d z}_{I_i(z_1,L,\zR)}
          - \underbrace{\int_{z_2}^{\infty} \frac{\fstab_i(z/L)[1-\frsl_i(z/\zR)]}{z} d z}_{I_i(z_2,L,\zR)}
        \label{eq:integralR}
\end{align}
with each of the integrals $I_i$ now depending on one less parameter.

To make estimation of $I_i$ more convenient, we can make a substitution $z' = z/\zR$; $z=\zR z'$
\begin{equation}
    I_i (z_1/\zR,\zR/L) =
        \int_{z_1/\zR}^\infty \fstab_i\left(\frac{\zR}{L}z'\right)\left[1-\frsl_i(z')\right]\frac{dz'}{z'}
    \label{eq:integralI}
\end{equation}
Expressed as a function of two parameters, this integral can feasibly be tabulated in a look-up table. Remembering that $\zeta_a = z_a/L$ (and therefore $L=z_a/\zeta_a$), $I_2$ can be expressed in terms of that variable if necessary. Figure~\ref{fig:integralIm} shows the integral $I_m$ --- with typical AM4/ESM4 $\fstab_m$ and Ghannam (2002) $\frsl_m$ --- as a function of $1/L$, $\zR/z_a$, and $z_a/\zR$ for some ranges of parameters. \todo[inline]{Calculations of $I_i$ vs $1/L$ take much longer for $\zR = \SI{1}{\m}$ (\SI{48}{\s} vs $<\SI{1}{s}$). It is not clear why: perhaps function is too sharp around zero? or relative precision canot be achieved, becase function values are too small?}

Parameter $z_1/\zR$ is always positive; $\zR/L$ can be either positive or negative, depending on sign of $L$.

When $z_1$ is close to zero, the function under the integral is not guaranteed to remain finite. Moreover, it may be proportional to $1/z$, which is easy to demonstrate in neutral case: $\fstab_i$ is 1, and $1-\frsl_i$ is also close to 1 when $z \to 0$. That means that when $z_1 \to 0$ the $I_{2,i} \to \infty$.

Figures~\ref{fig:integralIm} and~\ref{fig:integralIh} indicate that the integrals $I_i$ are fairly well-behaved along the $\zR/z_a$ axis, but they have very steep derivatives with respect to $1/L$ around zero. That means that to tabulate these integrals with high accuracy we would need either to reduce the step along $1/L$ near zero, or to make some appropriate substitution of variable to make them better behaved.
